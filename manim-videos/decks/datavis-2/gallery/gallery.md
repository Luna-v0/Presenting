## A. Chart toolbox

![Chart toolbox contact sheet](images/contact_sheet_a.png)

### Small multiples **(dropped)**

![Small multiples](images/a01_small_multiples.png)

- **What it shows:** Splits one chart into a grid of small, identically-scaled panels, one per category or facet.
- **Answers well:** Compares many groups at once without one cluttered overlay; the eye repeats the same read across panels.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use with more than ~12 panels, or when panels need different axis scales to be readable.
- **Call:** `sns.histplot(df, x=..., hue=..., ...)` faceted manually, or `sns.displot(df, x=..., col=...)`

### Violin / raincloud

![Violin / raincloud](images/a02_raincloud.png)

- **What it shows:** Shows the full distribution shape (violin/KDE) plus the raw points and quartiles, per group.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'what does the whole distribution look like, not just its mean', including multimodality a box plot hides.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use with very small samples (<~15 per group) — the KDE invents shape that isn't there.
- **Call:** `sns.violinplot(df, x=.., y=.., inner=None)` + `sns.stripplot(...)` overlaid

### ECDF **(dropped)**

![ECDF](images/a03_ecdf.png)

- **What it shows:** The empirical cumulative distribution: for each x, the fraction of points ≤ x. No binning choice to argue about.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'what fraction of the group is below/above a threshold' and compares distributions without histogram bin artefacts.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use with an audience unfamiliar with reading a cumulative curve without more framing/annotation.
- **Call:** `sns.ecdfplot(df, x=.., hue=..)`

### Slope chart **(dropped)**

![Slope chart](images/a04_slope_chart.png)

- **What it shows:** One line per entity connecting its value at two time points (or two conditions).
- **Answers well:** Answers 'who went up, who went down, by how much' between exactly two points — better than a grouped bar for that.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use with more than ~10-15 entities (the lines cross and the labels collide) or more than two time points.
- **Call:** `ax.plot([0,1], [before, after])` per row, drawn directly on a plain `matplotlib` axis

### Dumbbell **(dropped)**

![Dumbbell](images/a05_dumbbell.png)

- **What it shows:** A horizontal line between two dots per category — the two dots are the two values being compared, sorted by one of them.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'how far apart are these two values, for each of many categories', sorted so the biggest gaps are easy to spot.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use for more than ~2 points per category — a dumbbell is specifically a two-point comparison.
- **Call:** `ax.hlines(y, v1, v2)` then `ax.scatter(v1, y)` / `ax.scatter(v2, y)` on a plain `matplotlib` axis

### Ridgeline **(dropped)**

![Ridgeline](images/a06_ridgeline.png)

- **What it shows:** Stacked, overlapping KDE curves, one per category, offset vertically.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'how does the distribution shift and change shape across an ordered set of categories' (e.g. price by grade).
- **When not to use it:** Don't use with unordered categories (the stacking order becomes arbitrary and misleading) or more than ~8-10 rows.
- **Call:** `scipy.stats.gaussian_kde` per group, plotted with `ax.fill_between` at increasing offsets (no seaborn/matplotlib ridgeline primitive)

### Hexbin / 2D density **(dropped)**

![Hexbin / 2D density](images/a07_hexbin.png)

- **What it shows:** Bins a scatter into hexagonal cells and colours each by point count — a scatter plot that doesn't saturate into one black smear.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'where is the data actually dense' for two continuous variables with tens of thousands of points.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use for small n (<~1,000) — a plain scatter is clearer and binning adds nothing but noise.
- **Call:** `ax.hexbin(x, y, gridsize=40, cmap='viridis')`, or `sns.jointplot(df, x=.., y=.., kind='hex')`

### Bump chart **(dropped)**

![Bump chart](images/a08_bump_chart.png)

- **What it shows:** Like a slope chart but for rank, at more than two time points: one line per entity, y-axis is rank (1 = best).
- **Answers well:** Answers 'who was ahead of whom, and when did the order change' — rank over time, not the raw values.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use when the underlying values matter as much as the order — a bump chart hides magnitude entirely.
- **Call:** `ax.plot(years, ranks)` per entity on a plain `matplotlib` axis with an inverted y-axis

### Waffle **(needs `pywaffle`, not in seaborn/matplotlib/pandas/scipy; dropped)**

![Waffle](images/a09_waffle.png)

- **What it shows:** A part-to-whole chart as a grid of small squares, each worth one unit (or a fixed count), coloured by category.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'what share of the whole does each category hold', literally countable, for a small number of categories.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use for more than ~5-6 categories, or when precise comparison (not just 'roughly how much') matters — use a bar.
- **Call:** `pywaffle.Waffle` as a custom `matplotlib` Figure class: `plt.figure(FigureClass=Waffle, rows=.., values=..)`

### Treemap **(needs `squarify`, not in seaborn/matplotlib/pandas/scipy; dropped)**

![Treemap](images/a10_treemap.png)

- **What it shows:** A part-to-whole chart as nested/adjacent rectangles, area proportional to value.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'what share of the whole does each category hold' when there are too many categories for a pie or waffle to stay legible.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use when precise comparison matters (area is hard to compare precisely — Cleveland's ranking again) or categories are few enough for a bar chart.
- **Call:** `squarify.plot(sizes=.., label=..)` (no treemap primitive in seaborn/matplotlib)

### Sankey / flow **(dropped)**

![Sankey / flow](images/a11_sankey.png)

- **What it shows:** Shows a quantity splitting (or merging) from one state into several, width proportional to amount.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'where did the people/volume/money go' — a flow or funnel question, not a static comparison.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use for more than 2-3 stages by hand with `matplotlib.sankey` — it has no automatic layout, unlike a dedicated alluvial library.
- **Call:** `matplotlib.sankey.Sankey(ax=ax).add(flows=.., labels=.., orientations=..).finish()`

### Parallel coordinates **(dropped)**

![Parallel coordinates](images/a12_parallel_coordinates.png)

- **What it shows:** One vertical axis per variable, normalised to the same 0-1 range, one polyline per row connecting its values across all axes.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'do rows that are high on one variable tend to be high (or low) on another', across more than 2-3 variables at once.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use with more than ~6-8 variables or categories whose lines are indistinguishable once overplotted — the chart becomes visual noise.
- **Call:** `pandas.plotting.parallel_coordinates(df, class_column=..)`

### Calendar heatmap **(dropped)**

![Calendar heatmap](images/a13_calendar_heatmap.png)

- **What it shows:** A grid with weekday on one axis and week on the other, each cell shaded by a daily value.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'which days were unusual' and reveals weekly/seasonal rhythm that a plain time-series line smooths over.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use for less than a few weeks of data (too few cells to show a pattern) or more than ~2 years (cells get unreadably small).
- **Call:** `ax.imshow(grid, cmap=..)` on a week-by-weekday array built with `pandas` resample + reshape (no calendar-heatmap primitive in seaborn/matplotlib)

### Lollipop **(dropped)**

![Lollipop](images/a14_lollipop.png)

- **What it shows:** A bar chart with the bar replaced by a thin line and a dot at the tip.
- **Answers well:** Answers the same ranking question as a bar chart, with less ink — useful when there are many categories and a solid bar gets heavy.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use when the audience needs to read exact bar lengths quickly at a glance — a dot is a little harder to compare than a bar edge.
- **Call:** `ax.hlines(y, 0, value)` + `ax.scatter(value, y)` on a plain `matplotlib` axis

### Annotated time series **(dropped)**

![Annotated time series](images/a15_annotated_timeseries.png)

- **What it shows:** A line chart with a callout marking the specific point the chart is about.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'what happened, and when' by pointing at the moment that matters instead of leaving the audience to find it.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use more than 1-2 annotations per chart — past that they compete with each other and with the line.
- **Call:** `ax.annotate(text, xy=.., xytext=.., arrowprops=..)` on top of `ax.plot(...)`


## B. Principles (before / after)

![Principles contact sheet](images/contact_sheet_b.png)

### Choose the chart by the question

![Choose the chart by the question](images/b01_choose_by_question.png)

A line implies order/continuity between categories; species has neither. The bar makes no claim the data doesn't support.

### Cleveland's perceptual ranking

![Cleveland's perceptual ranking](images/b02_cleveland_ranking.png)

People read position on a common scale far more accurately than angle or area — the pie slices are genuinely hard to rank by eye; the bar is instant.

### Data-ink and decluttering

![Data-ink and decluttering](images/b03_data_ink.png)

Every mark that isn't carrying information (heavy grid, border, hatch, redundant axis lines) is a tax on the reader's attention; strip it.

### Colour choice

![Colour choice](images/b04_colour.png)

'jet' is not perceptually uniform (equal steps in value don't look equally far apart) and fails for colour-blind readers; a sequential map like viridis fixes both.

### Annotation

![Annotation](images/b05_annotation.png)

A chart states what happened; an annotation states why the reader should care about one point in particular, instead of leaving them to search for it.

### A title that states the takeaway

![A title that states the takeaway](images/b06_takeaway_title.png)

'Figure 1' tells the reader nothing; a title that states the finding lets them read the chart in the direction the presenter means it.

### Sorting

![Sorting](images/b07_sorting.png)

Alphabetical order answers 'what letter does the name start with', which is almost never the question; sorting by value makes the ranking the first thing seen.

### Aspect ratio

![Aspect ratio](images/b08_aspect_ratio.png)

The same line, stretched tall and narrow, reads as a much steeper trend than the identical line banked near 45°, the rule of thumb for an undistorted slope.


## C. How to lie with charts (misleading vs. honest)

![How-to-lie contact sheet](images/contact_sheet_c.png)

### Truncated y-axis

![Truncated y-axis](images/c01_truncated_axis.png)

Starting the y-axis above zero stretches small differences into what looks like a large gap. Spot it by checking where the axis actually starts; the honest version starts a bar axis at zero.

### Dual axes

![Dual axes](images/c02_dual_axes.png)

Two independent y-axes can always be rescaled until their lines overlap, making any two trends look locked together. Spot it by asking whether the two axes' ranges were chosen to make the lines match; the honest version puts both series on one shared, meaningful scale (e.g. indexed to 100).

### Area / pictogram scaling **(dropped)**

![Area / pictogram scaling](images/c03_area_scaling.png)

Scaling an icon's width/radius by the value makes its area grow with the square of the value, exaggerating the ratio. Spot it by checking whether width or area was scaled; the honest version scales area directly.

### Cherry-picked time window

![Cherry-picked time window](images/c04_cherry_picked_window.png)

A short window chosen because it happens to show a clean trend hides what the series does outside it. Spot it by asking why that start/end date, specifically; the honest version shows the full available range.

### Cumulative vs. per-period **(dropped)**

![Cumulative vs. per-period](images/c05_cumulative_vs_period.png)

A cumulative total can only go up even while the underlying per-period activity is slowing or falling, which is exactly how Apple's 2013 chart made slowing iPhone growth look unstoppable. Spot it by asking 'cumulative since when?'; the honest version plots the per-period value. Labelled in Apple's own fiscal quarters (FY runs Oct-Sep, one quarter ahead of the calendar) rather than relabelled to calendar quarters, so the axis matches the data it's actually plotting.

### 3D pie **(dropped)**

![3D pie](images/c06_3d_pie.png)

Perspective and the squashed ellipse of a 3D pie distort both the angle and the apparent area of each slice, on top of a pie's existing Cleveland problem. Spot it by the fake depth/shadow; the honest version is a flat 2D chart (and a bar is better still).

### Mean vs. median (skew)

![Mean vs. median (skew)](images/c07_mean_median.png)

On a right-skewed distribution the mean is pulled toward the few extreme high values and no longer describes a 'typical' case. Spot it by checking the distribution's shape; the honest version reports the median (or both, named).

### Simpson's paradox

![Simpson's paradox](images/c08_simpsons_paradox.png)

Pooling two groups with very different sizes and success rates can flip which treatment looks better, as in the classic Charig et al. (1986) kidney-stone data: open surgery wins in both the small-stone and large-stone groups, but PCNL wins pooled, because PCNL was used mostly on the easier small-stone cases. Spot it by asking whether a hidden grouping variable differs between the compared groups; the honest version reports the split.

### Correlation ≠ causation (spurious correlation)

![Correlation ≠ causation (spurious correlation)](images/c09_spurious_correlation.png)

Any two series that both trend monotonically over the same years will correlate strongly by construction, with no causal link between them (in the spirit of Tyler Vigen's spurious-correlations project, tylervigen.com — redrawn here with two real but unrelated datasets rather than his exact pairing, which we did not have verified numbers for). Spot it by asking whether a shared third cause (here, just time) explains both; the honest version shows both series indexed over time side by side instead of implying a relationship.

### Survivorship bias

![Survivorship bias](images/c10_survivorship_bias.png)

Studying only the aircraft that returned (Abraham Wald, WWII) means the sample has already excluded the planes hit in the fatal spots — the undamaged areas on survivors are exactly where a hit was fatal. Spot it by asking what didn't make it into the sample; the honest version reasons about the missing cases, not just the visible ones.

### Base-rate neglect **(backup)**

![Base-rate neglect](images/c11_base_rate_neglect.png)

A test's accuracy sounds like the chance a positive result is correct, but with a rare condition most positives are still false positives. Spot it by asking for the base rate before trusting a headline accuracy figure; the honest version works through the actual counts (or Bayes' rule).

### Misleading colour scales **(backup)**

![Misleading colour scales](images/c12_misleading_colour_scale.png)

A non-perceptually-uniform colormap like 'jet' creates visual bands and false edges that aren't in the underlying values — shown here on a synthetic, perfectly smooth field (sum of two Gaussian bumps, no real edges anywhere) so any banding visible is purely the colormap's doing. Spot it by looking for hard colour boundaries that don't match the data; the honest version uses a perceptually uniform sequential map (viridis, rocket, ...).

### Log scale without saying so **(backup)**

![Log scale without saying so](images/c13_log_scale.png)

A log y-axis compresses large values and makes accelerating growth look steady — not dishonest by itself, but misleading when the axis doesn't say it's log, especially with plain round-number tick labels that give no hint of the log spacing. Spot it by checking whether the tick values are evenly spaced (linear) or not (log); the honest version here plots the same series on a clearly labelled linear axis, where the true accelerating growth is visible.

### Binning tricks in histograms **(dropped)**

![Binning tricks in histograms](images/c14_binning_tricks.png)

Too few or oddly-placed histogram bins can hide real structure (like Old Faithful's two distinct eruption types) or invent structure that isn't there. Spot it by re-drawing with a different bin count/width and checking whether the shape survives; the honest version picks bins fine enough to show real structure (or uses an ECDF/KDE instead).


## D. Well-known charts (not covered in part 1)

![contact sheet](images/contact_sheet_d.png)

### Box vs. violin vs. strip

![Box vs. violin vs. strip](images/d01_box_violin_strip.png)

- **What it shows:** Three ways to draw the same distribution: a box (quartiles only), a violin (full shape via KDE), a strip (every raw point).
- **Answers well:** Answers 'what does this distribution look like' at three levels of detail — box for a quick comparison, violin for shape, strip for seeing the actual n and any gaps/clusters.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use a box alone when the shape matters (it can hide bimodality); don't use a strip alone with large n (overplots).
- **Call:** `sns.boxplot(...)`, `sns.violinplot(...)`, `sns.stripplot(...)` — three calls, same `x=`/`y=`

### Grouped bar

![Grouped bar](images/d02_grouped_bar.png)

- **What it shows:** Bars for one category, grouped side by side by a second category.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'how does the value compare across two categorical dimensions at once' — e.g. a metric by group and by sub-group.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use with more than ~3-4 sub-groups per cluster or many categories — the clusters get wide and hard to compare.
- **Call:** `sns.barplot(df, x=.., y=.., hue=..)`

### Stacked bar + 100% stacked bar

![Stacked bar + 100% stacked bar](images/d03_stacked_bar.png)

- **What it shows:** A bar split into coloured segments by a second category; the 100% version rescales every bar to the same height so segments show share, not raw total.
- **Answers well:** Stacked: answers 'what's the total, and its composition'. 100% stacked: answers 'what share does each part hold', comparable across categories with very different totals.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use stacked (non-100%) when the audience needs to compare a middle segment across bars — only the bottom segment and the total are easy to compare.
- **Call:** `pandas` `crosstab(...).plot(kind='bar', stacked=True)`, or `df.div(df.sum(axis=1), axis=0)` first for the 100% version

### Pie / donut

![Pie / donut](images/d04_pie_donut.png)

- **What it shows:** A circle split into wedges (or a ring, for donut) sized by share of the whole.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'what share of the whole does each part hold' — genuinely fine at 2-3 parts, where the comparison is obvious at a glance.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use past ~4-5 parts (this sample's 8-part version is already hard to rank by eye — see Cleveland's ranking, principle B2); use a bar instead.
- **Call:** `ax.pie(values, labels=.., wedgeprops={'width': 0.4})` (the `width` makes it a donut; omit for a plain pie)

### Area and stacked area

![Area and stacked area](images/d05_area.png)

- **What it shows:** A line chart with the area under it filled; stacked area layers several filled series so the top line is their running total.
- **Answers well:** Plain area: emphasises magnitude/volume over time more than a bare line. Stacked area: answers 'how does the total, and each part's contribution to it, change over time'.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use stacked area with more than ~5-6 series (the bands become hard to read individually) or with series that cross zero.
- **Call:** `ax.fill_between(x, y)` for plain area; `ax.stackplot(x, *series, labels=..)` for stacked

### Bubble chart

![Bubble chart](images/d06_bubble.png)

- **What it shows:** A scatter plot with a third continuous variable mapped to point size (and often a fourth to colour).
- **Answers well:** Answers 'how do three (or four) variables relate at once' for individual points — e.g. two measurements plus a magnitude.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use when the size differences are subtle (hard to compare area by eye — Cleveland again) or with too many overlapping points.
- **Call:** `sns.scatterplot(df, x=.., y=.., size=.., hue=.., sizes=(min,max))`

### Pair plot

![Pair plot](images/d07_pairplot.png)

- **What it shows:** A grid of scatter plots for every pair of chosen variables, with each variable's own distribution on the diagonal.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'how does every variable relate to every other one' in a single exploratory view — the first thing to run on a new numeric dataset.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use with more than ~5-6 variables (the grid gets too small per panel to read) or for a polished final slide — it's an exploration tool, not a takeaway chart.
- **Call:** `sns.pairplot(df, vars=[...], hue=..)`

### Correlation-matrix heatmap

![Correlation-matrix heatmap](images/d08_corr_heatmap.png)

- **What it shows:** A heatmap of pairwise correlation coefficients, with a diverging colour scale centred on zero, annotated values, and the redundant upper triangle masked out.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'which variables move together, and how strongly' across many variables at once, faster than scanning a pair plot.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use a sequential colormap here (correlation is signed, -1 to 1 — needs diverging, see principle B4) or skip the mask (the matrix is symmetric; showing both triangles doubles the ink for no new information).
- **Call:** `sns.heatmap(df.corr(), mask=np.triu(...), cmap='coolwarm', center=0, annot=True)`

### Error bars / confidence intervals

![Error bars / confidence intervals](images/d09_error_bars.png)

- **What it shows:** A point or bar estimate (here, a mean) with a line through it marking a confidence interval — the range the true value plausibly falls in, not the spread of the raw data.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'how sure are we about this estimate' — whether two groups' CIs overlap is a quick, honest check for whether a difference is likely real.
- **When not to use it:** Don't present a bar/point estimate without some error indicator when the audience might compare groups — and don't label the interval as 'the data's spread' (it isn't; that's a box/violin's job).
- **Call:** `sns.pointplot(df, x=.., y=.., errorbar=('ci', 95))`, or `sns.barplot(..., errorbar=...)`

### Count plot

![Count plot](images/d10_count_plot.png)

- **What it shows:** A bar chart where the bar height is just the count of rows in each category — seaborn builds the tally for you.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'how many of each category are there' straight from raw rows, without a manual `.value_counts()` first.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use when the quantity of interest isn't a row count (use `barplot` with an explicit `y=` for a computed statistic instead).
- **Call:** `sns.countplot(df, x=.., hue=..)`

### Radar / spider chart **(dropped)**

![Radar / spider chart](images/d11_radar.png)

- **What it shows:** Several normalised variables plotted as spokes around a circle, one polygon per entity.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'how does this entity's profile compare across several variables at once' — popular for comparing a handful of named things (players, products, species).
- **When not to use it:** Don't use with more than ~6-8 spokes (hard to read) or more than ~4-5 overlaid entities (the polygons occlude each other); and never to compare magnitude precisely — spoke length is easy to misjudge.
- **Call:** No seaborn/matplotlib radar primitive — build with `fig.add_subplot(polar=True)`, `ax.plot`/`ax.fill` on angles from `np.linspace(0, 2*pi, n, endpoint=False)`

### Pareto chart **(dropped)**

![Pareto chart](images/d12_pareto.png)

- **What it shows:** Bars sorted largest to smallest, with a line tracking the cumulative percentage of the total as you move left to right.
- **Answers well:** Answers the classic 80/20 question: 'how many categories account for most of the total' — where the cumulative line crosses 80% is the answer.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use when the categories have a natural order that isn't by size (e.g. time periods) — sorting breaks that order and the chart stops answering a real question.
- **Call:** `ax.bar(sorted_counts)` + `ax.twinx().plot(cumulative_pct)` — no single seaborn/matplotlib call does both

### Waterfall chart **(dropped)**

![Waterfall chart](images/d13_waterfall.png)

- **What it shows:** A bar chart where each bar starts where the running total left off, so positive and negative steps visibly build up to (or draw down from) a final total.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'what added to, and what subtracted from, this total, and by how much' — a budget, a bridge from one period's number to the next.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use when the steps aren't genuinely additive (they must sum to the start/end values) or there are more than ~6-8 steps (the bridge gets hard to follow).
- **Call:** `ax.bar(labels, heights, bottom=running_totals)` — no seaborn/matplotlib waterfall primitive

### Small multiples via FacetGrid/catplot

![Small multiples via FacetGrid/catplot](images/d14_facetgrid.png)

- **What it shows:** The seaborn-native way to build small multiples: pass `col=` (and/or `row=`) to a figure-level function and it builds the grid, shared axes, and legend for you.
- **Answers well:** Same question as A1's small multiples (compare many groups at once) — this is the idiomatic seaborn call instead of a hand-rolled `plt.subplots` loop.
- **When not to use it:** Don't reach for this over a hand-built grid when you need per-panel customisation the figure-level API doesn't expose (different chart types per panel, custom per-panel annotation).
- **Call:** `sns.catplot(df, x=.., y=.., col=.., kind='box')`, or `sns.displot(..., col=..)` for distributions


## E. Charts ML people already use

![contact sheet](images/contact_sheet_e.png)

### Confusion matrix **(needs `scikit-learn`, not in seaborn/matplotlib/pandas/scipy)**

![Confusion matrix](images/e01_confusion_matrix.png)

- **What it shows:** A grid of predicted class vs. true class counts (or row-normalised rates) for a classifier.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'which classes does the model actually confuse with which' — far more specific than a single accuracy number.
- **When not to use it:** Don't read counts alone when classes are imbalanced (a few errors on a rare class can be a bigger problem than many on a common one) — check the normalised version too.
- **Call:** `sklearn.metrics.confusion_matrix(y_true, y_pred)`, plotted with `sns.heatmap(cm, annot=True)`

### ROC and precision-recall curves **(needs `scikit-learn`, not in seaborn/matplotlib/pandas/scipy; dropped)**

![ROC and precision-recall curves](images/e02_roc_pr.png)

- **What it shows:** ROC plots true- vs. false-positive rate across every classification threshold; precision-recall plots precision vs. recall across the same thresholds.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'how good is this classifier across all thresholds, not just the default 0.5' — PR is the one to trust when the positive class is rare.
- **When not to use it:** Don't use ROC/AUC alone on a strongly imbalanced problem (it can look good even when precision is poor) — check precision-recall too.
- **Call:** `sklearn.metrics.roc_curve`/`auc`, `precision_recall_curve`/`average_precision_score`

### Feature-importance bar **(needs `scikit-learn`, not in seaborn/matplotlib/pandas/scipy; dropped)**

![Feature-importance bar](images/e03_feature_importance.png)

- **What it shows:** A sorted, horizontal bar of how much each input feature contributed to a fitted model's predictions.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'what is the model actually using to decide' — a first, rough check before trusting or explaining a model.
- **When not to use it:** Don't treat built-in importances as a causal explanation, and don't use them to compare models of different types (the scales aren't comparable); consider permutation importance for a fairer comparison.
- **Call:** `clf.feature_importances_` (tree models), plotted with `ax.barh(sorted_features, sorted_importances)`

### Learning curve **(needs `scikit-learn`, not in seaborn/matplotlib/pandas/scipy; dropped)**

![Learning curve](images/e04_learning_curve.png)

- **What it shows:** Train and validation score plotted against training-set size.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'would this model improve with more data, or is it already maxed out' — and whether the gap between the two lines signals overfitting.
- **When not to use it:** Don't run it on a dataset too small to leave a meaningful validation fold, and don't read a single run's noise as a trend — average over folds (as `learning_curve`'s cross-validation already does).
- **Call:** `sklearn.model_selection.learning_curve(estimator, X, y, train_sizes=.., cv=..)`

### Residual plot **(needs `scikit-learn`, not in seaborn/matplotlib/pandas/scipy; dropped)**

![Residual plot](images/e05_residuals.png)

- **What it shows:** Predicted value on the x-axis, (actual - predicted) on the y-axis, for a fitted regression.
- **Answers well:** Answers 'is the model's error random, or is there a pattern left over' — a fan shape, curve, or trend in the residuals means the model is missing something systematic.
- **When not to use it:** Don't skip this for a regression chart that only shows predicted vs. actual — a residual plot makes a systematic pattern far easier to spot than the same information folded into a 45° scatter.
- **Call:** `residuals = y_true - model.predict(X)`, plotted with `ax.scatter(model.predict(X), residuals)`
